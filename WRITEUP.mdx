At one million documents, tuned Postgres full-text search matches Elasticsearch on p99 latency for plain-term and phrase queries and beats it on throughput. The one place Postgres actually loses is typo tolerance, which it does not have at all — that gap, not raw speed, is the real reason teams reach for Elasticsearch.

## Question

The author's scar is a real Postgres-to-Elasticsearch migration that bought a large latency win. The question worth answering isn't "which engine is faster" — it's at what corpus size and query shape Postgres FTS actually stops being enough, since "just use Elasticsearch" is advice that costs a team an ops budget it may not need.

## Setup

Three single-node engines, one seeded corpus, indexed one at a time so their indexes never coexist on disk:

- **PostgreSQL 17** (Alpine), stored generated `tsvector` + GIN index, `shared_buffers=2GB`, `work_mem=64MB`, `maintenance_work_mem=1GB`, `random_page_cost=1.1`, `ANALYZE` after load, ranked with `ts_rank_cd`.
- **Elasticsearch 8.19.5**, single shard, 0 replicas, 2 GB heap, refresh disabled during bulk load then force-merged to one segment.
- **Meilisearch v1.24**, `MEILI_ENV=production`, documents pushed in 20k batches.

The corpus (`cmd/bakeoff/corpus.go`) is generated from a fixed seed with marker tokens **planted into known document ids**, so relevance is known by construction rather than assumed: rare-term, two-term AND, and phrase queries each have an exact 10-document relevant set that a test (`corpus_test.go`) verifies actually holds in the generated text. The `common` query shape (a token present in ~5% of the corpus) has thousands of true positives, so recall@10 there is **NOT MEASURED** — an unjudged number would be worse than none. `typo` queries corrupt one character of a planted rare token; rare tokens are built so every one is at edit distance ≥3 from every other, so a fuzzy matcher can't be accidentally right.

Machine: Apple M1 Pro, 8 cores, 16 GB RAM, macOS, Docker via OrbStack capped at 8 CPU/8 GB. All three engines are single-node; nothing here says anything about clustering. Each arm ran under a shared host-wide lock (load + query, since build time is a reported number) with 4 repetitions per query shape, first discarded as warm-up, concurrency 8.

## What I expected

Elasticsearch clearly ahead on latency at any real corpus size, Postgres competitive only on toy data, and Meilisearch fastest but with the biggest index footprint.

## What happened

Corpus size actually indexed: **N = 1,000,000 documents** (not 10M — three simultaneous indexes plus load-generation overhead didn't fit the ~20 GiB free on this shared machine; see Threats to validity). Postgres was competitive with Elasticsearch on every shape it supports, and lost only where it structurally cannot compete: typo tolerance.

Meilisearch was fastest on every measured shape but its index is roughly 4x the corpus size on disk and its build time (36 minutes at 1M docs, mostly per-batch document processing) was by far the longest of the three — the opposite of its reputation as the "instant" engine, at least under this uninstrumented batch-write path.

Postgres's `common`-shape p99 (1.5s) is the standout bad number: ranking a query that matches 5% of a million-row table with `ts_rank_cd` is expensive, and it's exactly the shape real full-text workloads hit constantly (the top handful of terms in any corpus).

## Numbers

Corpus: 1,000,000 documents, 81 judged queries across 5 shapes, 4 reps (first discarded).

| Engine              | Build time | Index size | rare p99 | and p99 | phrase p99 | typo                   | common p99 |
| ------------------- | ---------- | ---------- | -------- | ------- | ---------- | ---------------------- | ---------- |
| Postgres 17 (tuned) | 5m 50s     | 1.83 GiB   | 13.4ms   | 15.9ms  | 9.8ms      | **unsupported**        | 1490ms     |
| Elasticsearch 8.19  | 1m 6s      | 456 MiB    | 10.6ms   | 10.5ms  | 15.3ms     | 20.1ms (recall@10 1.0) | 31.7ms     |
| Meilisearch 1.24    | 36m 34s    | 7.43 GiB   | 7.6ms    | 20.5ms  | 5.1ms      | 13.7ms (recall@10 1.0) | 15.0ms     |

Recall@10 / MRR@10 were 1.000 / 1.000 for every engine on every judged shape (`rare`, `and`, `phrase`) it supports — the planted ground truth was retrievable by all three at this corpus size. `common` is NOT MEASURED (too many true positives for a top-10 cutoff to mean anything). Full raw JSON, per-query-shape p50/p95/max, throughput, and container RSS are in `results/n1000000/*.json` and `*.rss.txt`.

"Index size" is not measured identically across engines and that is worth stating plainly: Elasticsearch and Meilisearch's numbers are `du -sk` on their entire data directory, which is effectively index-only for a single-purpose container. Postgres's number is `pg_total_relation_size('docs')` — the table plus its GIN index plus TOAST — deliberately excluding WAL and the rest of the cluster's system catalogs, which would otherwise inflate a shared-cluster database's footprint against two engines that don't have that overhead. The whole-directory `du -sk` for the same Postgres container was 3.46 GiB (committed in `results/n1000000/postgres.du.txt`) — the gap between that and the reported 1.83 GiB is exactly WAL plus catalog overhead, not a discrepancy.

Steady-state RSS: Postgres 797 MiB, Elasticsearch 3.0 GiB (of its 2 GB heap plus off-heap/Lucene cache), Meilisearch 1.35 GiB. No engine was pushed to a memory failure point — this is not a ceiling, just steady state after the load.

## Why

Postgres's GIN-indexed tsvector does an excellent job on bounded-cardinality lookups — rare term, two-term AND, phrase — because those all resolve to small posting-list intersections, which is exactly what a GIN index is built for. It falls over on `common` because ranking a huge candidate set with `ts_rank_cd` means scoring hundreds of thousands of rows per query; Elasticsearch and Meilisearch both use algorithms (BM25 with skip-list-accelerated top-k retrieval) designed to avoid fully scoring a match set that large. And Postgres has no notion of edit-distance matching in its FTS stack at all — `typo` isn't slow for it, it's absent.

## Decision

See [`docs/adr/0001-postgres-fts-threshold.md`](docs/adr/0001-postgres-fts-threshold.md): use tuned Postgres FTS below roughly a few million rows as long as the product does not need typo/fuzzy matching and query volume on high-frequency terms is not the dominant workload; move to Elasticsearch (or Meilisearch, with its build-time and disk tradeoffs) once either of those two conditions is hit, not because of a generic "Postgres doesn't scale" belief.

## Threats to validity

Corpus size is 1M documents, not the 10M originally targeted — this machine had ~20 GiB free while running six concurrent unrelated experiments, and one engine's index alone (Meilisearch, 7.4 GiB) would have left no room for a second arm at 10M. The trend (Postgres's `common`-shape p99 degrading as candidate-set size grows) is visible but not confirmed past 1M; a team near that ceiling should re-run `make bench N=<their scale>` rather than trust extrapolation. All three engines are single-node — nothing here speaks to replica behavior, failover, or horizontal scaling, which is a large part of the real migration argument for Elasticsearch. The corpus is synthetic Zipfian word-salad, not real natural-language text, so absolute latency numbers (not the relative ordering or the typo-support gap) may not transfer to a real corpus with real term-frequency skew and stopword handling. Memory was not pushed to a failure ceiling. Queries ran from the same host as the containers over loopback, avoiding real network latency. Postgres's `common` p99 was measured at concurrency 8 on a table without partitioning or a covering index tuned specifically for that access pattern — a team hitting this wall has tuning options not attempted here (e.g. a separate index or materialized ranking) before reaching for Elasticsearch.
